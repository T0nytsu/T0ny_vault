## ST §2.3 Differentiation Formulas

> [!note] Note
> The facts that the answers use, each under its name. An answer writes the rule itself; the names are those of ST §2.3.
> - **Constant**: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(c) = 0$.
> - **[[Power Rule]]**: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^n) = nx^{n-1}$ for every real number $n$ (general version). Write roots and reciprocals as powers first: $\sqrt[k]{x^m} = x^{m/k}$, $\dfrac{1}{x^n} = x^{-n}$.
> - **Constant Multiple, Sum and Difference Rules**: $(cf)' = cf'$, $(f + g)' = f' + g'$, $(f - g)' = f' - g'$.
> - **[[Product Rule]]**: $f$, $g$ differentiable $\implies (fg)' = fg' + gf'$. Not $f'g'$.
> - **[[Quotient Rule]]**: $f$, $g$ differentiable $\implies \left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)' = \dfrac{gf' - fg'}{g^2}$ where $g \ne 0$. The denominator goes first in the numerator.
> - **Tangent line** at $(a, f(a))$: $y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)$. **[[Normal Line|Normal line]]**: the line through the same point perpendicular to the tangent line, slope $-\dfrac{1}{f'(a)}$ when $f'(a) \ne 0$.
> - Parallel lines have equal slopes; horizontal tangent $\iff f'(x) = 0$.
> - **[[One-Sided Derivative|Left- and right-hand derivatives]]** (ST §2.2): $f'(a)$ exists $\iff f'_{-}(a) = f'_{+}(a)$; and differentiable $\implies$ continuous.
> - **Derivative with $x \to a$** (Equation 2.1.5): $f'(a) = \lim_{x \to a} \dfrac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}$.
> - Each answer is written as on an exam paper: the computation first, one $=$ per line, then the conclusion. A paragraph that starts with \# is a remark and is not part of the answer.

### ST 2.3.7
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.3 Questions#ST 2.3.7|ST 2.3.7]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> f'(x) &= \tfrac{3}{2}x^{3/2 - 1} + (-3)x^{-3 - 1} \\
> &= \tfrac{3}{2}x^{1/2} - 3x^{-4}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> \# Power Rule on each term, then the Sum Rule. The exponent goes down by $1$ also when it is negative: $-3 - 1 = -4$, not $-2$. Both $\tfrac{3}{2}x^{1/2} - 3x^{-4}$ and $\tfrac{3}{2}\sqrt{x} - \dfrac{3}{x^4}$ are fine as an answer.

### ST 2.3.13

> \begin{aligned}
> g(x) &= x^{-1/2} + x^{1/4} \\

### ST 2.3.23
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.3 Questions#ST 2.3.23|ST 2.3.23]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> P(w) &= \frac{2w^2}{w^{1/2}} - \frac{w}{w^{1/2}} + \frac{4}{w^{1/2}} = 2w^{3/2} - w^{1/2} + 4w^{-1/2} \\

### ST 2.3.27
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.3 Questions#ST 2.3.27|ST 2.3.27]]

> [!solution] Solution
> **Product Rule**
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> f'(x) &= (1 + 2x^2)(x - x^2)' + (x - x^2)(1 + 2x^2)' \\
> &= (1 + 2x^2)(1 - 2x) + (x - x^2)(4x) \\
> &= 1 - 2x + 2x^2 - 4x^3 + 4x^2 - 4x^3 \\
> &= 1 - 2x + 6x^2 - 8x^3
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> **Multiplication first**
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> f(x) &= x - x^2 + 2x^3 - 2x^4 \\
> f'(x) &= 1 - 2x + 6x^2 - 8x^3
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> Yes, the answers agree.
>
> \# The two ways must give the same polynomial, so comparing them checks the expansion. For two polynomial factors, multiplying first is often faster; the Product Rule is needed when a factor has no formula, as $g(x)$ in [[#ST 2.3.81]].

### ST 2.3.35
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.3 Questions#ST 2.3.35|ST 2.3.35]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> g'(t) &= \frac{(5t + 1)(3 - 2t)' - (3 - 2t)(5t + 1)'}{(5t + 1)^2} \\
> &= \frac{(5t + 1)(-2) - (3 - 2t)(5)}{(5t + 1)^2} \\
> &= \frac{-10t - 2 - 15 + 10t}{(5t + 1)^2} \\
> &= -\frac{17}{(5t + 1)^2}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> \# Denominator times the derivative of the numerator, minus the numerator times the derivative of the denominator; the other order gives the opposite sign. The $t$-terms always cancel for $\dfrac{at + b}{ct + d}$, whose derivative is $\dfrac{ad - bc}{(ct + d)^2}$: here $a = -2$, $b = 3$, $c = 5$, $d = 1$, and $ad - bc = -2 - 15 = -17$.

### ST 2.3.43
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.3 Questions#ST 2.3.43|ST 2.3.43]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> H(u) &= u^2 - (\sqrt{u})^2 = u^2 - u \\
> H'(u) &= 2u - 1
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> \# $(a - b)(a + b) = a^2 - b^2$ removes the roots at once. The Product Rule gives the same result after more work: $(u - \sqrt{u})\left(1 + \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{u}}\right) + (u + \sqrt{u})\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{u}}\right) = \left(u - \dfrac{\sqrt{u}}{2} - \dfrac{1}{2}\right) + \left(u + \dfrac{\sqrt{u}}{2} - \dfrac{1}{2}\right) = 2u - 1$. Because of $\sqrt{u}$, $H$ is defined only for $u \ge 0$, so the formula holds for $u > 0$.

### ST 2.3.45

### ST 2.3.49
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.3 Questions#ST 2.3.49|ST 2.3.49]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> G'(y) &= \frac{(Ay^3 + B)(B)' - B(Ay^3 + B)'}{(Ay^3 + B)^2} \\
> &= \frac{(Ay^3 + B) \cdot 0 - B \cdot 3Ay^2}{(Ay^3 + B)^2} \\
> &= -\frac{3ABy^2}{(Ay^3 + B)^2}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> \# The variable is $y$; $A$ and $B$ are constants, so $(B)' = 0$ and $(Ay^3 + B)' = 3Ay^2$. A constant over a function always gives $\left(\dfrac{c}{g}\right)' = -\dfrac{cg'}{g^2}$, the Reciprocal Rule of ST 2.3.108 times $c$.

### ST 2.3.51
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.3 Questions#ST 2.3.51|ST 2.3.51]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> f(x) &= \frac{x}{x + \dfrac{c}{x}} \cdot \frac{x}{x} = \frac{x^2}{x^2 + c} && (x \ne 0) \\
> f'(x) &= \frac{(x^2 + c)(2x) - x^2(2x)}{(x^2 + c)^2} \\
> &= \frac{2x^3 + 2cx - 2x^3}{(x^2 + c)^2} \\
> &= \frac{2cx}{(x^2 + c)^2}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> \# Clear the small fraction first by multiplying the numerator and the denominator by $x$; then one Quotient Rule. $c$ is a constant. $f$ is not defined at $x = 0$ (because of $\dfrac{c}{x}$), so the formula holds for $x \ne 0$ with $x^2 + c \ne 0$.

### ST 2.3.53

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> P'(x) = na_n x^{n-1} + (n - 1)a_{n-1}x^{n-2} + \cdots + 2a_2x + a_1

### ST 2.3.61
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.3 Questions#ST 2.3.61|ST 2.3.61]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> y' &= 1 + \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} \\
> y'(1) &= 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{3}{2}
> \end{aligned}
> $$

### ST 2.3.63

### ST 2.3.69
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.3 Questions#ST 2.3.69|ST 2.3.69]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> f'(x) &= \frac{(1 + 2x)(2x) - x^2(2)}{(1 + 2x)^2} = \frac{2x^2 + 2x}{(1 + 2x)^2}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> $(1 + 2x)^2 = 4x^2 + 4x + 1$, so $\left[(1 + 2x)^2\right]' = 8x + 4 = 4(1 + 2x)$.
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> f''(x) &= \frac{(1 + 2x)^2(4x + 2) - (2x^2 + 2x) \cdot 4(1 + 2x)}{(1 + 2x)^4} \\
> &= \frac{(1 + 2x)\left[2(1 + 2x)^2 - 4(2x^2 + 2x)\right]}{(1 + 2x)^4} && (4x + 2 = 2(1 + 2x)) \\
> &= \frac{2(4x^2 + 4x + 1) - (8x^2 + 8x)}{(1 + 2x)^3} \\
> &= \frac{2}{(1 + 2x)^3}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> \# Without the Chain Rule (ST §2.5), the derivative of $(1 + 2x)^2$ comes from expanding it. Then take out the common factor $1 + 2x$ and cancel one power before simplifying. Everything holds for $x \ne -\tfrac{1}{2}$.

### ST 2.3.79

> [!solution] Solution

> &g(1) = 3,\ \ g'(1) = 1 && (g \text{ through } (0, 2) \text{ and } (2, 4)) \\
> &f(4) = 3,\ \ f'(4) = \tfrac{1}{3} \\
> &g(4) = 2,\ \ g'(4) = 1 && (g \text{ through } (3, 1) \text{ and } (5, 3))
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> **(a)**
> $$
> u'(1) = f(1)g'(1) + g(1)f'(1) = 2 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} = 3
> $$
> **(b)**
> $$
> v'(4) = \frac{g(4)f'(4) - f(4)g'(4)}{[g(4)]^2} = \frac{2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} - 3 \cdot 1}{2^2} = \frac{-\tfrac{7}{3}}{4} = -\tfrac{7}{12}
> $$
>
> ![ST 2.3.83 figure|460](../Attachments/CAL1%20HW%202.3%20Solutions%20fig%2083.png)
>
> \# Read slopes from two grid points on the same segment: $f$ rises $1$ for every $3$ to the right, $g$ rises $1$ for every $1$. The numbers $1$ and $4$ are not at corners ($f$ turns at $-1$, $g$ at $2$ and $3$), so $u'(1)$ and $v'(4)$ exist.

### ST 2.3.107
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.3 Questions#ST 2.3.107|ST 2.3.107]]

> [!proof] Proof
> **(a)** $f$ and $g$ are differentiable, so $fg$ is differentiable by the Product Rule. Use the Product Rule for $(fg) \cdot h$, then for $fg$:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> (fgh)' &= \left[(fg)h\right]' = (fg)'h + (fg)h' \\
> &= (f'g + fg')h + fgh' \\
> &= f'gh + fg'h + fgh'
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> **(b)** Take $f = g = h$ in (a):
> $$
> \frac{d}{dx}[f(x)]^3 = f'(x)f(x)f(x) + f(x)f'(x)f(x) + f(x)f(x)f'(x) = 3[f(x)]^2 f'(x) \qquad \blacksquare
> $$

> [!solution] Solution
> **(c)** By (b) with $f(x) = x^4 + 3x^3 + 17x + 82$, $f'(x) = 4x^3 + 9x^2 + 17$:
> $$
> y' = 3(x^4 + 3x^3 + 17x + 82)^2(4x^3 + 9x^2 + 17)
> $$
>
> \# (b) is the first case of the Chain Rule (ST §2.5): differentiate the cube as if the inside were $x$, then multiply by the derivative of the inside. Expanding $(x^4 + 3x^3 + 17x + 82)^3$ first would take much longer.

### ST 2.3.111
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.3 Questions#ST 2.3.111|ST 2.3.111]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $f(1) = 1 + 1 = 2$.
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> f'_{-}(1) &= \lim_{h \to 0^{-}} \frac{f(1 + h) - f(1)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^{-}} \frac{\left[(1 + h)^2 + 1\right] - 2}{h} && (1 + h < 1) \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0^{-}} \frac{2h + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^{-}} (2 + h) = 2 \\[6pt]
> f'_{+}(1) &= \lim_{h \to 0^{+}} \frac{f(1 + h) - f(1)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^{+}} \frac{\left[(1 + h) + 1\right] - 2}{h} && (1 + h > 1) \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0^{+}} \frac{h}{h} = 1
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> $\because f'_{-}(1) = 2 \ne 1 = f'_{+}(1)$, $\therefore f$ is not differentiable at $1$.
> $$
> f'(x) = \begin{cases} 2x & \text{if } x < 1 \\ 1 & \text{if } x > 1 \end{cases}
> $$
> ![ST 2.3.111 figure|560](../Attachments/CAL1%20HW%202.3%20Solutions%20fig%20111.png)
>
> \# $f$ is continuous at $1$ (both pieces give $2$), so the only problem is the corner. At $1$ itself use the definition with the correct piece on each side; away from $1$ each piece is a polynomial, so the rules give $(x^2 + 1)' = 2x$ and $(x + 1)' = 1$. $f'(1)$ does not exist, so the graph of $f'$ has open dots at $x = 1$.

### ST 2.3.115
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.3 Questions#ST 2.3.115|ST 2.3.115]]

> [!solution] Solution
> For $x < 2$, $f'(x) = 2x$; for $x > 2$, $f'(x) = m$. It remains to make $f$ differentiable at $2$.
>
> (1) Differentiable $\implies$ continuous at $2$:
> $$
> \lim_{x \to 2^{-}} f(x) = 4 = f(2), \quad \lim_{x \to 2^{+}} f(x) = 2m + b \implies 2m + b = 4
> $$
> (2) Equal one-sided derivatives at $2$:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> f'_{-}(2) &= \lim_{h \to 0^{-}} \frac{(2 + h)^2 - 4}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^{-}} (4 + h) = 4 \\
> f'_{+}(2) &= \lim_{h \to 0^{+}} \frac{\left[m(2 + h) + b\right] - 4}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^{+}} \frac{(2m + b - 4) + mh}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^{+}} \frac{mh}{h} = m && \text{(by (1))}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> $f'_{-}(2) = f'_{+}(2) \implies m = 4$, and then $b = 4 - 2m = -4$.
>
> Hence $m = 4$ and $b = -4$.
>
> \# Two unknowns need two conditions: the pieces meet (continuity) and there is no corner (equal one-sided derivatives). Without (1) the right-hand quotient $\dfrac{(2m + b - 4) + mh}{h}$ goes to $\pm\infty$. Check: $y = 4x - 4$ is the tangent line of $y = x^2$ at $(2, 4)$.

### ST 2.3.119
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.3 Questions#ST 2.3.119|ST 2.3.119]]

> [!solution] Solution
> Let $f(x) = x^{1000}$, so $f(1) = 1$.
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^{1000} - 1}{x - 1} &= \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{f(x) - f(1)}{x - 1} = f'(1) \\
> f'(x) &= 1000x^{999} \implies f'(1) = 1000
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> The limit is $1000$.
>
> \# The limit is the definition of $f'(1)$ in the form with $x \to a$ (Equation 2.1.5). Without it, one needs $x^{1000} - 1 = (x - 1)(x^{999} + x^{998} + \cdots + x + 1)$: $1000$ terms, each $\to 1$; this is the first proof of the Power Rule. The exercise was also a CYCU midterm problem (term 107-1).

### CYCU 2025.1.10
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.3 Questions#CYCU 2025.1.10|CYCU 2025.1.10]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right)^{-1} &= \left(\frac{x + 1}{x}\right)^{-1} = \frac{x}{x + 1} && (x \ne 0,\ -1) \\
> \left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right)^{-1} + 5 &= \frac{x + 5(x + 1)}{x + 1} = \frac{6x + 5}{x + 1} \\
> f(x) &= \frac{x + 1}{6x + 5} && \left(x \ne -\tfrac{5}{6}\right) \\
> f'(x) &= \frac{(6x + 5)(1) - (x + 1)(6)}{(6x + 5)^2} = -\frac{1}{(6x + 5)^2}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> \# $A^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{A}$: turn the compound fraction into one quotient, then use the Quotient Rule once. The official answer key leaves the answer as $-\dfrac{1}{\left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right)^2\left[\left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right)^{-1} + 5\right]^2 x^2}$, which is the same function: $\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{x}\right)^2 x^2 = (x + 1)^2$ and $\left[\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{x}\right)^{-1} + 5\right]^2 = \dfrac{(6x + 5)^2}{(x + 1)^2}$.

## UW Math 124 Midterm Exam

### UW 2025.1.2 (d)
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.3 Questions#UW 2025.1.2 (d)|UW 2025.1.2 (d)]]

> [!solution] Solution
> Let $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x + 3}$; then $f(x + h) = \dfrac{1}{x + 3 + h}$ and
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{1}{x + 3 + h} - \frac{1}{x + 3}}{h} &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} = f'(x) \\
> &= \frac{(x + 3) \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1}{(x + 3)^2} = -\frac{1}{(x + 3)^2}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> \# Recognize $\dfrac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}$, the definition of $f'(x)$. The direct way gives the same: $\dfrac{(x + 3) - (x + 3 + h)}{h(x + 3 + h)(x + 3)} = \dfrac{-1}{(x + 3 + h)(x + 3)} \to -\dfrac{1}{(x + 3)^2}$.
