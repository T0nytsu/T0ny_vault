## ST §2.5 The Chain Rule

> [!note] Note
> The facts that the answers use, each under its name. An answer writes the formula itself.
> - **[[Chain Rule]]**: if $g$ is differentiable at $x$ and $f$ is differentiable at $g(x)$, then $(f \circ g)'(x) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$. In Leibniz notation, $y = f(u)$, $u = g(x)$: $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{du}\dfrac{du}{dx}$. Work from the outside to the inside: the derivative of the outer function, evaluated at the inner function, times the derivative of the inner function.
> - **Power Rule with the Chain Rule**: $\dfrac{d}{dx}[g(x)]^n = n[g(x)]^{n - 1}g'(x)$ for every real number $n$.
> - **Trigonometric functions with the Chain Rule** ($u$ a differentiable function of $x$): $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin u = \cos u\,\dfrac{du}{dx}$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos u = -\sin u\,\dfrac{du}{dx}$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan u = \sec^2 u\,\dfrac{du}{dx}$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sec u = \sec u\tan u\,\dfrac{du}{dx}$.
> - **Product and Quotient Rules**: $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$, $\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)' = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}$.
> - **[[Tangent Line]]** at $(a, f(a))$: $y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)$. Horizontal tangent $\iff f'(a) = 0$.
> - Each answer is written as on an exam paper: the outer derivative first, then $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\text{inner})$, one $=$ per line. A paragraph that starts with \# is a remark and is not part of the answer.

### ST 2.5.7
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.5 Questions#ST 2.5.7|ST 2.5.7]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> f'(x) &= 5(2x^3 - 5x^2 + 4)^4 \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(2x^3 - 5x^2 + 4) \\
> &= 5(2x^3 - 5x^2 + 4)^4(6x^2 - 10x) \\
> &= 10x(3x - 5)(2x^3 - 5x^2 + 4)^4
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> \# Outer function $u^5$, inner function $u = 2x^3 - 5x^2 + 4$. The Power Rule alone gives only $5(\cdots)^4$; the factor $6x^2 - 10x$ is the derivative of the inner function, and it is the part that is easy to forget. Do not expand $(\cdots)^4$.

### ST 2.5.17

> \begin{aligned}

### ST 2.5.31
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.5 Questions#ST 2.5.31|ST 2.5.31]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> y &= \cos x\,(1 + \sin x)^{-1/2} \\
> y' &= -\sin x\,(1 + \sin x)^{-1/2} + \cos x \cdot \left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)(1 + \sin x)^{-3/2}\cos x \\
> &= -\tfrac{1}{2}(1 + \sin x)^{-3/2}\left[2\sin x(1 + \sin x) + \cos^2 x\right] \\
> &= -\tfrac{1}{2}(1 + \sin x)^{-3/2}\left[2\sin x + 2\sin^2 x + 1 - \sin^2 x\right] && (\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x) \\
> &= -\tfrac{1}{2}(1 + \sin x)^{-3/2}(1 + \sin x)^2 \\
> &= -\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{1 + \sin x}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> \# Writing $y$ as a product with a negative power avoids the Quotient Rule. The key step is $\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x$: the bracket becomes $\sin^2 x + 2\sin x + 1 = (1 + \sin x)^2$, and almost everything cancels. The Quotient Rule gives the same answer with one more line.

### ST 2.5.47

### ST 2.5.61
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.5 Questions#ST 2.5.61|ST 2.5.61]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> f'(x) &= 2\cos x + 2\sin x\cos x = 2\cos x\,(1 + \sin x) \\
> f'(x) = 0 &\iff \cos x = 0 \ \text{ or } \ \sin x = -1 \\
> &\iff \cos x = 0 && (\sin x = -1 \implies \cos x = 0) \\
> &\iff x = \tfrac{\pi}{2} + n\pi,\ n \in \mathbb{Z} \\[4pt]
> x = \tfrac{\pi}{2} + 2n\pi &: \ f(x) = 2 \cdot 1 + 1^2 = 3 \\
> x = \tfrac{3\pi}{2} + 2n\pi &: \ f(x) = 2(-1) + (-1)^2 = -1
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> Hence the tangent line is horizontal at the points $\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} + 2n\pi, 3\right)$ and $\left(\tfrac{3\pi}{2} + 2n\pi, -1\right)$, $n$ an integer.
>
> \# Horizontal tangent $\iff f'(x) = 0$. $\sin^2 x = (\sin x)^2$, so the Chain Rule gives $2\sin x\cos x$; factor it with the first term instead of writing $\sin 2x$. "All points" means every period: $f$ has period $2\pi$, so add $2n\pi$. The heights $3$ and $-1$ alternate, so split $\tfrac{\pi}{2} + n\pi$ into the two cases.

### ST 2.5.67
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.5 Questions#ST 2.5.67|ST 2.5.67]]

> [!solution] Solution
> From the graph (each piece is a line segment, slope $= \dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}$):
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> &g:\ \ g(1) = 4,\quad g'(1) = g'(2) = -1 \ \ (\text{segment } (0, 5) \text{ to } (3, 2)),\quad g'(4) = \tfrac{1}{2} \ \ (\text{segment } (3, 2) \text{ to } (7, 4)) \\

> \begin{aligned}
> \text{(a)}\ \ u'(1) &= f'(g(1))\,g'(1) = f'(4)\,g'(1) = \left(-\tfrac{1}{4}\right)(-1) = \tfrac{1}{4} \\
> \text{(b)}\ \ v'(1) &= g'(f(1))\,f'(1) = g'(2)\,f'(1) = (-1)(2) = -2 \\
> \text{(c)}\ \ w'(1) &= g'(g(1))\,g'(1) = g'(4)\,g'(1) = \tfrac{1}{2}(-1) = -\tfrac{1}{2}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> All three exist: the corners of $f$ are at $0$ and $2$, the corners of $g$ at $0$ and $3$, and none of the numbers where $f'$ or $g'$ is evaluated ($4$ for $f'$; $1$, $2$, $4$ for $g'$; $1$ for $f'$) is a corner of that function.
>
> ![ST 2.5.67 figure|460](../Attachments/CAL1%20HW%202.5%20Solutions%20fig%2067.png)
>
> \# The graph gives two kinds of numbers: heights (the inner values $g(1)$, $f(1)$) and slopes (the derivatives). A derivative read from a graph like this fails to exist only at a corner, so before computing, check where each derivative is evaluated. If, for example, $g(1)$ were $3$, then $w'(1) = g'(3)\,g'(1)$ would not exist, because $g$ has a corner at $3$.

### ST 2.5.75
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.5 Questions#ST 2.5.75|ST 2.5.75]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> D\cos 2x &= -2\sin 2x \\
> D^{2}\cos 2x &= -4\cos 2x \\
> D^{3}\cos 2x &= 8\sin 2x \\
> D^{4}\cos 2x &= 16\cos 2x = 2^{4}\cos 2x \\[4pt]
> \implies D^{4k}\cos 2x &= 2^{4k}\cos 2x \\
> 103 = 4 \cdot 25 + 3 \implies D^{103}\cos 2x &= D^{3}\left(2^{100}\cos 2x\right) = 2^{100} \cdot 8\sin 2x = 2^{103}\sin 2x
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> \# Each derivative multiplies by $2$ (the derivative of the inner $2x$), and the trigonometric part cycles $\cos \to -\sin \to -\cos \to \sin \to \cos$ with period $4$. So only $103$ modulo $4$ matters for the trigonometric part, and the number in front is $2^{103}$.

### ST 2.5.83
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.5 Questions#ST 2.5.83|ST 2.5.83]]

> [!proof] Proof
> Let $f$ be differentiable. By the Chain Rule with inner function $-x$,
> $$
> \frac{d}{dx}f(-x) = f'(-x) \cdot (-1) = -f'(-x).
> $$
> **(a)** $f$ even: $f(-x) = f(x)$ for all $x$. Differentiate both sides:
> $$
> -f'(-x) = f'(x) \implies f'(-x) = -f'(x) \implies f' \text{ is odd.}
> $$
> **(b)** $f$ odd: $f(-x) = -f(x)$ for all $x$. Differentiate both sides:
> $$
> -f'(-x) = -f'(x) \implies f'(-x) = f'(x) \implies f' \text{ is even.} \qquad \blacksquare
> $$
>
> \# The same statement was proved from the definition in [[CAL1 HW 2.2 Solutions#ST 2.2.61|ST 2.2.61]], with the substitution $k = -h$. The Chain Rule turns that computation into one line: the inner function $-x$ has derivative $-1$. Both sides of $f(-x) = f(x)$ are the same function of $x$, so their derivatives are equal.

### WEB 2.5.1
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.5 Questions#WEB 2.5.1|WEB 2.5.1]]

> [!proof] Proof
> Chain Rule: if $u = g(x)$ is differentiable at $a$ and $y = f(u)$ is differentiable at $b = g(a)$, then $F = f \circ g$ is differentiable at $a$ and $F'(a) = f'(g(a))\,g'(a)$.
>
> Let $\Delta x$ be an increment in $x$, $\Delta u = g(a + \Delta x) - g(a)$, and $\Delta y = f(b + \Delta u) - f(b) = F(a + \Delta x) - F(a)$.
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> &g \text{ is differentiable at } a:\ \ \text{let } \varepsilon_1 = \frac{\Delta u}{\Delta x} - g'(a) \ (\Delta x \ne 0),\ \ \varepsilon_1 = 0 \ (\Delta x = 0) \\
> &\implies \Delta u = \left[g'(a) + \varepsilon_1\right]\Delta x, \quad \varepsilon_1 \to 0 \text{ as } \Delta x \to 0 \\[4pt]
> &f \text{ is differentiable at } b:\ \ \text{let } \varepsilon_2 = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta u} - f'(b) \ (\Delta u \ne 0),\ \ \varepsilon_2 = 0 \ (\Delta u = 0) \\
> &\implies \Delta y = \left[f'(b) + \varepsilon_2\right]\Delta u, \quad \varepsilon_2 \to 0 \text{ as } \Delta u \to 0 \\
> &\phantom{\implies}\ (\text{if } \Delta u = 0, \text{ then } \Delta y = f(b) - f(b) = 0 \text{ and both sides are } 0)
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> Substitute $\Delta u$ into $\Delta y$:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \Delta y &= \left[f'(b) + \varepsilon_2\right]\left[g'(a) + \varepsilon_1\right]\Delta x \\
> \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} &= \left[f'(b) + \varepsilon_2\right]\left[g'(a) + \varepsilon_1\right] && (\Delta x \ne 0)
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> As $\Delta x \to 0$: $\Delta u = \left[g'(a) + \varepsilon_1\right]\Delta x \to 0$, so $\varepsilon_1 \to 0$ and $\varepsilon_2 \to 0$. Hence
> $$
> F'(a) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \left[f'(b) + \varepsilon_2\right]\left[g'(a) + \varepsilon_1\right] = f'(b)\,g'(a) = f'(g(a))\,g'(a). \qquad \blacksquare
> $$
>
> \# Why not simply $\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta u} \cdot \dfrac{\Delta u}{\Delta x}$? Because $\Delta u$ can be $0$ while $\Delta x \ne 0$ (for example when $g$ is constant near $a$), and we cannot divide by $0$. The form $\Delta y = [f'(b) + \varepsilon_2]\Delta u$ never divides by $\Delta u$, and setting $\varepsilon_2 = 0$ when $\Delta u = 0$ makes $\varepsilon_2$ continuous at $0$; that is what lets "$\Delta u \to 0$" carry over to "$\varepsilon_2 \to 0$". This is the textbook's proof at the end of §2.5 (Equations 5, 6 and 7 there).

### WEB 2.5.2
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.5 Questions#WEB 2.5.2|WEB 2.5.2]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> f'(x) &= 2\tan(\sec^4(x^2 + 1)) \cdot \sec^2(\sec^4(x^2 + 1)) \cdot \frac{d}{dx}\sec^4(x^2 + 1) \\
> \frac{d}{dx}\sec^4(x^2 + 1) &= 4\sec^3(x^2 + 1) \cdot \sec(x^2 + 1)\tan(x^2 + 1) \cdot 2x = 8x\sec^4(x^2 + 1)\tan(x^2 + 1) \\
> \implies f'(x) &= 16x\tan(\sec^4(x^2 + 1))\sec^2(\sec^4(x^2 + 1))\sec^4(x^2 + 1)\tan(x^2 + 1)
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> \# Five links: $(\ \cdot\ )^2 \to \tan \to (\ \cdot\ )^4 \to \sec \to x^2 + 1$. Because an answer without work must be exactly right, count the numbers at the end: $2 \cdot 4 \cdot 2x = 16x$, and $\sec^3 \cdot \sec = \sec^4$.

### WEB 2.5.3

> [!solution] Solution

### WEB 2.5.4
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.5 Questions#WEB 2.5.4|WEB 2.5.4]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> f'(x) &= -2\sin x + 2\cos 2x \\
> f'(x) = 0 &\iff \cos 2x = \sin x \\
> &\iff 1 - 2\sin^2 x = \sin x \\
> &\iff 2\sin^2 x + \sin x - 1 = 0 \\
> &\iff (2\sin x - 1)(\sin x + 1) = 0 \\
> &\iff \sin x = \tfrac{1}{2} \ \text{ or } \ \sin x = -1 \\
> &\iff x = \tfrac{\pi}{6},\ \tfrac{5\pi}{6},\ \tfrac{3\pi}{2} && (0 < x < 2\pi) \\[4pt]
> f\left(\tfrac{\pi}{6}\right) &= 2 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \sin\tfrac{\pi}{3} = \tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} \\
> f\left(\tfrac{5\pi}{6}\right) &= 2\left(-\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) + \sin\tfrac{5\pi}{3} = -\tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} \\
> f\left(\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\right) &= 2 \cdot 0 + \sin 3\pi = 0
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> Hence the points are $\left(\tfrac{\pi}{6}, \tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$, $\left(\tfrac{5\pi}{6}, -\tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$ and $\left(\tfrac{3\pi}{2}, 0\right)$.
>
> \# $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin 2x = 2\cos 2x$ by the Chain Rule. To solve $\cos 2x = \sin x$, write everything with $\sin x$ ($\cos 2x = 1 - 2\sin^2 x$) and get a quadratic in $\sin x$. The question asks for points, so give both coordinates.

### WEB 2.5.5
Question: [[CAL1 HW 2.5 Questions#WEB 2.5.5|WEB 2.5.5]]

> [!solution] Solution
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \text{(a)}\ \ \frac{dy}{dx} &= \sin x + x\cos x \\
> \text{(b)}\ \ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} &= \cos x + \cos x - x\sin x = 2\cos x - x\sin x \\
> \frac{d^3y}{dx^3} &= -2\sin x - \sin x - x\cos x = -3\sin x - x\cos x \\
> \text{(c)}\ \ \frac{d^4y}{dx^4} &= -3\cos x - \cos x + x\sin x = -4\cos x + x\sin x
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> **(d)** Pattern: $\dfrac{d^ny}{dx^n} = n\,(\text{the } (n-1)\text{th derivative of } \sin x) + x\,(\text{the } n\text{th derivative of } \sin x)$, and the derivatives of $\sin x$ repeat with period $4$: $\sin x$, $\cos x$, $-\sin x$, $-\cos x$.
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> 35 = 4 \cdot 8 + 3:\ \ &\text{the } 35\text{th derivative of } \sin x = -\cos x, \ \text{ the } 34\text{th} = -\sin x \\
> \frac{d^{35}y}{dx^{35}} &= 35(-\sin x) + x(-\cos x) = -35\sin x - x\cos x
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> \# Every derivative adds one more copy of the derivative of $\sin x$ (from the Product Rule), so the number in front counts $1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$, while the trigonometric parts cycle with period $4$. Check the pattern against (a)–(c) before using it. Exercise 76, $D^{35}\,x\sin \pi x$, is the Chain Rule version: each derivative also multiplies by $\pi$.
