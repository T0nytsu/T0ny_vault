## Stage 1: Line and Ellipse Control

This is the "Foundations" node from the roadmap, expanded into an actual curriculum. One assumption before I start: I'm building this around **35–45 minutes a day, 7 days a week** (~4–5 hrs/week). If your real capacity is different, tell me and I'll rescale volume — the exercises and order don't change, only how many reps you do.

### Why this comes first, specifically

Everything after this stage — boxes, perspective, gesture, anatomy — is expressed _through_ lines and curves. If your hand can't yet put a line where you intend it, at the speed and pressure you intend, then every future mistake is ambiguous: you won't know if a bad box is a **perspective error** (you don't understand the 3D logic) or an **execution error** (you understood it fine, your hand just didn't cooperate). Those two problems have completely different fixes, and trying to solve both at once wastes effort. Stage 1 exists to eliminate execution error as a variable before you ever touch 3D form. Nothing here is about "seeing" or observation yet — it's pure motor calibration.

Two skills, and only two: **confident lines** and **confident ellipses**. No boxes, no perspective, no gesture. Those are Stage 2.

### Materials

- **Plain printer paper**, a full ream. You will burn through it — that's the point.
- **A fineliner or ballpoint pen** (0.3–0.5mm), not a pencil. This isn't a style preference: a pencil invites small corrective scratches, which is exactly the habit this stage exists to remove. A pen forces you to see the _actual_ result of a committed stroke, which is the feedback this whole stage runs on.
- **A ruler** — for checking your work afterward, never for drawing the practice lines themselves.
- **A practice log** (a notebook page or notes app is fine): date, what you did, rough count, one observation. You'll need this in three weeks to compare against your first pages — that comparison is your main progress signal, not a subjective feeling of "getting better."

If your endgame is digital work eventually — fine, but do this stage on paper regardless. Undo removes the discomfort that makes the calibration work.

### Reading and video

Being direct: there isn't much _to read_ for this stage. It's a physical skill, and physical skills are better shown than described. Rather than pad this with a book that doesn't fit, here's what's actually worth your time:

- **Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling, _How to Draw_** — read **Chapter 1 only** ("Drawing Materials and Skills"). It covers exactly this: line confidence, controlled curves, freehand ellipses, drawing an ellipse on its minor axis. Stop there. Chapter 2 onward is perspective grids and vanishing points — that's Stage 2, and reading ahead now will just make you impatient with this stage.
- **Proko's free "Drawing Basics" series** — specifically the lessons "How to Hold and Control Your Pencil," "13 Types of Lines and How to Use Them," the line-quality lesson covering ghosting and rotating the paper, and the ellipses warm-up lesson. All free, all short, watch once before you start and don't rewatch daily — this is a doing stage, not a watching stage.
- **Drawabox.com, Lesson 1** — sections 1.1–2.1 only (Superimposed Lines, Ghosted Lines, Ghosted Planes, Table of Ellipses). I'm using their exercise names below because they're the clearest, most battle-tested versions of these drills. I'm deliberately _not_ having you do their boxes/perspective sections (1.3 onward gets perspective-adjacent) — that's Stage 2 material, kept out on purpose.

### The exercises

**Ghosted lines** — Mark two points. Hover the pen just above the page and sweep the intended path 3–5 times without touching down (this is "ghosting" — rehearsing the motion before committing). Pivot from the shoulder/elbow, not the wrist, for anything over about an inch — wrist-only produces an arc, not a straight line. Then commit: one continuous stroke from point to point, no stopping, no lifting, no going back to fix it.

- _Self-check:_ lay a ruler against the line and note the worst gap in mm. Track this number weekly — it should shrink. Also check: does it read as one stroke, or several overlapping ones?
- _Common mistakes:_ multiple short strokes instead of one commit; wrist-only motion; slowing down mid-line "to be careful" (this causes _more_ wobble, not less); stopping short of the endpoint.
- _Misconception:_ slower isn't more accurate here. A brisk, consistent-speed stroke is usually straighter than a hesitant one.

**Superimposed lines** — Ghost the same intended path as above, then execute 5–8 full committed passes over it (not a fast scribble — each pass is its own ghosted, deliberate stroke), aiming for the passes to converge into one clean line rather than fan out.

- _Self-check:_ do the passes tighten into a single line, or spread into a widening sheaf?
- _Common mistake:_ treating it as scribbling instead of discrete passes; wrist drift causing a curve as passes accumulate.

**Table of ellipses** — Draw a rough grid (4 rows × 5 columns). In each cell, circle the intended shape 2–3 times _above_ the page to build rotational momentum, then commit to one confident pass, closing exactly where you started.

- _Self-check:_ draw the long axis through a finished ellipse — do both halves mirror each other? Does it close without a gap or an overlapping tail?
- _Common mistakes:_ two-arc "seam" ellipses that don't blend into one loop; egg-shaping (one end pointier — asymmetric); going slow and getting a lumpy, faceted shape instead of a smooth curve.
- _Misconception:_ "it's just a squashed circle" — the symmetry-and-clean-closure requirement is genuinely harder than it looks, which is why it's drilled in isolation now rather than learned later while also fighting perspective.

### Stage 1 mastery checklist — this is your gate, not a calendar date

- [ ] Lines of varying length read as one confident stroke, minimal wobble, in at least 8 of 10 attempts.
- [ ] Ellipses close cleanly and are symmetrical along the long axis, at several different degrees (not just your favorite one).
- [ ] You can commit to a mark without immediately wanting to fix it.
- [ ] Your most recent practice page shows a clear, visible difference from your Day 1 page, side by side.

If any box is unchecked at the end of Week 3, don't start Stage 2 — repeat Week 3's structure for another week. Stage 2 assumes this motor vocabulary is automatic; starting it while you're still fighting your own hand just compounds the difficulty.

## Week 1 — building the motion

|Day|Warm-up|Lines|Ellipses|Time|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|1|Loose arm circles, 2 min|Ghosted lines, 2 pages, short + medium lengths|Freehand circles (not true ellipses yet) — just the spinning motion, 1 page|~30 min|
|2|Ghosted lines review, 10 lines|Introduce **superimposed lines**, 8 sets; ghosted lines, 1 page|Circles/loose ellipses, 1 page|~40 min|
|3|Same|Ghosted lines, 2 pages, add diagonals; superimposed lines, 1 page|Introduce **table of ellipses** (grid), 1 page, keep them wide/round|~45 min|

|5|Same|Ghosted lines, 2 pages; superimposed, 1 page|Table of ellipses, 1 page|~45 min|
|6|Same|Ghosted lines, 2 pages, push toward full-page length|Table of ellipses, 1 page, start varying degree column to column|~45 min|
|7|—|Redo 1 fresh page|Redo 1 fresh page|~40 min, then place both next to Day 1's pages. Write one sentence in the log describing what actually changed.|

That's roughly 14–15 hours total across three weeks. If Day 21 doesn't clear the checklist, that's not a failure — it means repeat Week 3's structure for another week before reassessing. There's no fixed calendar date for this; there's only the checklist.

We stop here. No perspective, no boxes, no gesture, no anatomy — that's Stage 2, and I'm not starting it until you tell me you've cleared the checklist above.
