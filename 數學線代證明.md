To prove these properties, we must look at how the dot product (inner product) is defined for vectors.
Let v be a vector in an n-dimensional space, represented by its components:
$$v = (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)$$ 
By definition, the dot product of a vector with itself (v ⋅ v) is the sum of the squares of its components:
$$v \cdot v = v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \dots + v_n^2$$ 
------------------------------
## Part 1: Proving v ⋅ v ≥ 0 (Positive-Semidefiniteness)

   1. Analyze the components: For any real number $v_i$, its square is always greater than or equal to zero ($v_i^2 \geq 0$).
   2. Sum the components: Since every single term in the sum $v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \dots + v_n^2$ is non-negative, their total sum must also be non-negative.
   3. Conclusion: Therefore, v ⋅ v ≥ 0.

------------------------------
## Part 2: Proving $v \cdot v = 0 \iff v = 0$ (Definiteness)
This is an "if and only if" (iff) statement, meaning we must prove it in both directions.
## Direction 1: If v = 0, then v ⋅ v = 0

   1. If v is the zero vector, then all of its components are zero ($v_1 = 0, v_2 = 0, \dots, v_n = 0$).
   2. Substitute these zeros into the dot product formula:
   $$v \cdot v = 0^2 + 0^2 + \dots + 0^2 = 0$$ 

## Direction 2: If v ⋅ v = 0, then v = 0

   1. Assume the sum of the squares is zero:
   $$v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \dots + v_n^2 = 0$$ 
   2. Because a squared real number can never be negative ($v_i^2 \geq 0$), the only way a sum of non-negative numbers can equal exactly zero is if every single term is individually zero:
   $$v_1^2 = 0, \quad v_2^2 = 0, \quad \dots, \quad v_n^2 = 0$$ 
   3. Taking the square root of both sides for each component gives:
   $$v_1 = 0, \quad v_2 = 0, \quad \dots, \quad v_n = 0$$ 
   4. Since all components are zero, v must be the zero vector (v = 0).
